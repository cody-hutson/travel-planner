# Tokyo Transport Brief — Alex's Group, Jul 15–22
### James Taggart | Tokyo, 18 years

---

## Arrival: Narita to Shintomicho (Jul 15, ~4:00 PM)

**My call: N'EX to Tokyo Station, then one taxi.**

Here's the maths. Four adults, presumably 4–8 bags, arriving at 4 PM on a Wednesday — that's the edge of rush hour. The Narita Express (N'EX) departs roughly every 30 minutes, takes 60 minutes to Tokyo Station, has dedicated luggage space above and beside the seats, and costs ¥3,070 per person with reserved seating. You book four seats together in one car, everyone sits down in air conditioning, and an hour later you're at Tokyo Station. From Tokyo Station, you take a single taxi to the hotel — roughly ¥1,500–2,000 for the four of you together, a 10-minute ride. Total door-to-door cost per person: roughly ¥3,500. Total time: ~80 minutes.

**Why not the Limousine Bus?** I know it sounds appealing — luggage underneath, no transfers, sits you near Ginza. But at 4 PM on a weekday, the expressway from Narita into central Tokyo can turn a 90-minute trip into 2.5 hours without warning. I've seen it happen in July, when a single thunderstorm backs up the Higashi-Kanto Expressway for an hour. You're sitting in a bus with no recourse. You also can't buy IC cards on the bus, so you arrive without the cards you need. The N'EX gets you on rails immediately, which in Japan means a predictable schedule.

**Why not a private transfer?** A reputable door-to-door van transfer (Narita to Shintomicho) runs ¥22,000–28,000. Split four ways, that's about ¥6,000 per person — nearly double the N'EX route. For a family with four young children and twelve bags I'd say yes. For four relatively mobile adults, no.

**At Narita, before you do anything else:** Exit customs, and before you even think about transport, stop at the JR East travel service counter or the green IC card machines near the N'EX ticket gates. This is where you sort your Suica cards (see below). Ticket machines take credit cards. Buy the cards first, then book N'EX tickets — you can do both at the same kiosk.

## IC Cards: The Only Thing That Matters Before Day One

**My call: Suica. Full stop.**

Suica and Pasmo are functionally identical — both work on every metro line, every JR line in Tokyo, every bus, and as contactless payment at most convenience stores and vending machines. The reason I say Suica is that if anyone in your group has a relatively recent iPhone or Apple Watch, they can load a **Mobile Suica** directly into Apple Wallet. No physical card. Top up with a credit card from your phone. Tap your watch to the reader. This is genuinely the slickest way to move through Tokyo, and it works perfectly for American tourists with iPhones.

**For those without iPhones or who prefer a physical card:** Get a **Welcome Suica** at the airport. It costs no deposit (unlike a regular Suica which has a ¥500 refundable deposit), is valid for 28 days from issue, and cannot be refunded — but since you're only here seven nights, that's irrelevant. Load ¥3,000–5,000 per card to start. You can top up at any ticket machine, any convenience store (7-Eleven, FamilyMart, Lawson), or on your phone.

**Practically:** Get four cards. The machines have English menus. Each card is individual — you can't share. The partner's mother should have her own card so she never has to fumble with cash or wait for someone to top up for her.

**One critical note:** Tap in, tap out. Every. Single. Time. If you forget to tap out on a metro ride and then try to tap in somewhere else, you'll get blocked at the gate and need to go to a station staff window. It happens to everyone once. Now it won't happen to you.

## JR Pass: Save Your Money

**My honest assessment: Don't buy it.**

The 7-day JR Pass currently costs around ¥50,000 per person — roughly $330 at today's rates. To justify that, each of you would need to spend ¥7,100 per day on JR-operated services. In Tokyo, you will not do that. The Tokyo Metro and Toei Subway cover the vast majority of tourist destinations. JR lines in the city (the Yamanote loop, the Chuo, Sobu) are useful but you'd pay ¥200–300 per ride — you'd have to make 15–20 JR journeys per person per day to break even. That's not a holiday, that's a commute.

Now, the day trip question. This is where people assume the JR Pass earns its keep. Let me go through each option:

- **Nikko:** The smart route is the **Tobu Line from Asakusa** — not JR. The JR route to Nikko exists but it's slower, more complicated, and requires a shinkansen connection. Tobu Pass covers most of what you need. JR Pass useless here.
- **Kamakura:** This *is* JR — Yokosuka Line from Tokyo Station, about 60 minutes. Return fare is roughly ¥1,890 per person. Multiply by four, that's ¥7,560 round trip for the group. Against a ¥50,000 JR Pass per person. Still miles off.
- **Hakone:** The best route is the **Odakyu Romancecar from Shinjuku** — not JR. Or the shinkansen to Odawara is JR, but then you'd need Hakone Tozan Railway anyway. The **Hakone Free Pass** (Odakyu) covers nearly everything you'll use there. JR Pass barely touches it.

**Verdict:** Buy IC cards. Use the metro like a local. Save ¥200,000 collectively.

The one exception worth mentioning: if you decide late in the trip to add a Kyoto extension, you'd book shinkansen tickets individually at that point. Still cheaper than four JR Passes.

## Daily Navigation

**Use Google Maps.** I know, I know — there are apps designed specifically for Japanese transit (Hyperdia, Navitime, the Japan Official Travel App). They're fine. But Google Maps has caught up significantly and has one killer advantage: it combines walking, metro, and real-world timing in a single interface that everyone in your group already knows how to use. When you're standing at Ginza-itchome at noon in 34°C heat and someone's mother needs to get to a rest stop, you don't want to learn a new app. You want to type your destination, tap "Directions," and walk.

**Key lines from your hotel:**

The hotel at Shintomicho is genuinely well-placed. Here's what you need to know cold:

- **Yurakucho Line** (1 minute walk from hotel): Your primary line. Runs northeast–southwest. Two stops west gets you to Yurakucho Station, which is steps from Hibiya Park, the Imperial Palace East Gardens, and transfers to JR lines. One stop east or west and you're in Ginza-itchome.
- **Hibiya Line** (6 minutes walk to Hatchobori): Runs the full tourist spine. Tsukiji (1 stop), Ginza (2 stops), Hibiya/Kasumigaseki (4 stops, Imperial grounds), Roppongi (7 stops), Naka-meguro (9 stops). This line is a lifeline.
- **Tsukiji Outer Market** is a 10-minute walk from the hotel. You don't need transit.

**Tokyo Metro 24/48/72-hour passes:** Worth serious consideration. ¥600/¥850/¥1,000 per person. If you're doing more than three metro trips in a day (which you will be, especially in July when you'll dip underground to escape the heat), the 24-hour pass pays for itself. Buy at any station kiosk. Note these cover Tokyo Metro only — not Toei Subway or JR. For most tourist days, that's fine.

## Taxis: When to Use Them and How

Tokyo taxis are clean, air-conditioned, honest (meters are law), and the drivers are polite to the point of ceremony. The door opens automatically — don't touch it, you'll confuse everyone. Starting fare is around ¥500–550, then ¥100 per 255 metres after that. A 2km crosstown hop costs you ¥800–1,200. A longer 5km ride is ¥1,500–2,500.

**Download the GO app** before you arrive (or shortly after). It's the dominant taxi-hailing app in Tokyo, English-friendly, accepts credit cards, and shows you a live map of nearby cabs. S.Ride is the other one — also fine, also English. Either works; GO has broader coverage.

**When to take a taxi:**

1. **Anywhere with luggage** — metro stairs with four suitcases is miserable. Always taxi with bags.
2. **When someone is overheating** — this is important for the partner's mother. If she's flagging at 1 PM in Ueno, get in a cab immediately. Don't walk to the nearest station, don't figure out which exit, don't wait on a platform. Hail a cab or open GO. ¥1,500 is nothing for a medical non-incident.
3. **After midnight** — taxis add a 20–30% night surcharge but it's still reasonable and the metro stops around midnight anyway.
4. **Wet evenings** — getting four people under an umbrella on a rainy metro platform isn't fun. Taxi from dinner is ¥1,000 and worth it.
5. **Anywhere involving "just three more stops with one transfer" at 9 PM** when everyone's tired.

The one thing to know: taxi drivers in Tokyo speak almost no English. Have your destination written in Japanese, or just show the map on your phone pointed at your hotel. The address in Japanese for your hotel is helpful to screenshot and keep accessible.

## Day Trips: My Recommendation for Your Group

With a heat-sensitive older adult, here's my honest ranking:

**Best choice: Kamakura.**

It's an hour from Tokyo Station on a comfortable JR train with reserved seating options. The main sights (Kotoku-in Great Buddha, Hase-dera temple, the shoreline) are reasonably walkable with taxis available between them. The coastal breeze in Kamakura is real — it's genuinely 2–3°C cooler than central Tokyo in July. Go early (be on the 8:30 AM train), hit the main sites while temperatures are manageable, retreat to a restaurant for a long lunch in AC, and be back in Tokyo by 3 PM having done it properly. Round trip is ¥1,890 per person on JR — book seats on the Yokosuka Line or Shonan-Shinjuku Line.

**Second choice: Hakone.**

Higher elevation means meaningfully cooler temperatures — often 5–8°C cooler than Tokyo. If you get Fuji views, it's spectacular. The Hakone Free Pass (¥4,000 from Shinjuku, covers everything) is excellent value. However: the Romancecar from Shinjuku takes 85 minutes, and getting around Hakone involves mountain railways, ropeways, and lake boats — which is wonderful but logistically more involved. Also, in early-to-mid July you're at the tail end of rainy season and Fuji will be clouded out about 70% of the time. If the views are your draw and it's overcast, it's a long trip for a lake and some hot springs.

**Skip Nikko for this group.** The Tosho-gu shrine complex is genuinely extraordinary and I'd send anyone else there. But it involves a two-hour train journey each way and then substantial uphill walking between shrines in July heat at relatively high humidity. The partner's mother will not enjoy this. Full stop.

**Day trip timing rule:** Whatever you choose, plan for a morning departure (8:00–9:00 AM). Do the outdoor elements before noon. Be underground, indoors, or back at the hotel by 1–2 PM. Every day in Tokyo in July, treat 11 AM–3 PM as the danger zone. Plan around it.

## Departure: Jul 22 for a 6:00 PM NRT Flight

**Leave the hotel at 12:30 PM. Not 1:00 PM. 12:30.**

Here's the calculation: For an international departure from Narita, you want to be at the terminal no later than 3:30 PM — that gives you 2.5 hours, which is comfortable for check-in, security, and a proper lunch airside. N'EX from Tokyo Station to Narita is 60 minutes. Getting from Shintomicho to Tokyo Station (Yurakucho Line, Hibiya Line, or a short taxi) takes 20–25 minutes. Add 15 minutes of buffer for luggage assembly and hotel checkout.

12:30 PM hotel departure → ~1:00 PM at Tokyo Station → 1:30 PM N'EX → 2:30 PM Narita Terminal. Ideal.

**Why not the Limousine Bus on departure?** Exactly the same reason as arrival. Wednesday afternoon isn't peak traffic but summer Narita expressway is unpredictable. N'EX is a fixed schedule on dedicated tracks. If you miss a Limousine Bus slot due to traffic and you're at Narita at 4:30 for a 6 PM flight, you're running. Don't do it.

**One thing to sort the night before:** If you've accumulated shopping — and you will, especially if anyone goes near Ginza or Tokyu Hands — you may have a bag weight problem. Japanese convenience stores and post offices offer a same-day luggage forwarding service (takkyubin) to the airport. If you're checking in heavy bags, you can send them from the hotel directly to the Narita check-in counter by 10 AM for delivery same afternoon. Not necessary but worth knowing if someone bought a rice cooker.
